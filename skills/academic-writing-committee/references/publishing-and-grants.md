## Venue Selection

Choose the venue before drafting, because it sets length, structure, style, and audience.

**Fit comes first.**

- Scope: has the venue published work on this question, with this kind of method, in the last two or three years?
- Audience: who needs to read this, and what do they read?
- Article type: does the venue have a type that suits the work (full paper, short paper, registered report, review, methods, data paper)?
- Policy: are its open-access route, licensing, data rules, and preprint policy compatible with the funder's and the institution's requirements?

**Then quality and practicality.**

- Indexing in the databases the field uses; a named editorial board with relevant expertise; a clear peer-review process.
- Time to first decision and to publication; acceptance rate where published; costs.
- Reputation among people in the field. Treat journal impact factors and similar metrics as weak evidence about a venue and as no evidence about a single paper.

**Model the venue.** Read three to five recent papers of the same type from it and note the section sequence, the length of each part, how the contribution is framed, voice, citation density, and figure style. This is more reliable than general assumptions about a field.

**Venue cultures differ.** Conferences in computing reward novelty and move fast; journals expect completeness. Theory venues expect proofs; applied and industry venues expect evidence of use. Humanities venues expect engagement with debates. Adapt the framing, not the facts.

Keep a ranked shortlist of two or three venues, so that a rejection leads to a planned next step instead of a rewrite from scratch.

## Screening for Predatory Venues

Warning signs: unsolicited, flattering invitations; promises of acceptance or review within days; fees that are unclear or disclosed only after acceptance; an editorial board that cannot be verified or whose members do not know they are on it; a title imitating an established journal; false claims of indexing or impact factors; a scope that covers everything.

Checks: the Think. Check. Submit. checklist; listing in the Directory of Open Access Journals for open-access journals; presence in the indexes your field relies on; membership of the publisher in COPE or a comparable body; the journal's actual published articles. The list once kept by Jeffrey Beall has been offline since 2017; do not rely on copies of it. Treat the warning signs as a screen, not a verdict: new legitimate journals show some of them. Ask a librarian when unsure.

## The Venue Policy Record

Before drafting and again before submitting, record the following from the venue's current instructions, with the address and the date:

- Article type; limits on words, abstract, references, figures, tables, and supplements.
- Required structure, reporting guideline, and checklists.
- Citation style and file formats; template version.
- Review model (single-anonymous, double-anonymous, or open; double-anonymous review is also called double-blind) and what must be anonymized.
- Policies on preprints, prior presentation, and duplicate submission.
- Authorship, contribution, and acknowledgment rules; how author changes are handled.
- Ethics, consent, registration, and image rules.
- Data, code, and materials policy and accepted repositories.
- Funding and competing-interest disclosures.
- AI-use rules for authors.
- Open-access options, license choices, fees, and waivers.
- Accessibility requirements such as alt text.

Generic advice never overrides this record. If a policy is absent or unclear, ask the editorial office; do not guess.

## Preprints

A preprint is a public version posted before or alongside journal review. It establishes a date for the work, makes it citable and open to comment early, and is free to read.

- Check the target venue's preprint policy first. Most accept preprinted work; a few restrict it, and double-anonymous venues may limit publicity during review.
- Choose the server for the field: arXiv (physics, mathematics, computing, statistics, quantitative fields), bioRxiv (biology), medRxiv (health sciences), ChemRxiv (chemistry), SSRN (social sciences, law, economics), SocArXiv (social sciences), PsyArXiv (psychology), EarthArXiv (earth sciences), engrXiv (engineering), PhilArchive and PhilSci-Archive (philosophy), or an institutional or general repository.
- Servers moderate submissions and set their own rules: on article types, on first-time submitters, on clinical research (medRxiv asks for trial registration and ethics statements), and on licenses. Read the server's current submission guide.
- Choose the license deliberately; it usually cannot be withdrawn.
- A preprint is permanent. Post a version you are prepared to stand behind, update it with new versions, and link it to the published article.
- Cite preprints as preprints, and say in your own preprint's metadata when it has been published.
- Posting a preprint does not by itself meet a funder's requirement for public access to the peer-reviewed version.

## Persistent Identifiers

| Identifier | Identifies | Use |
|---|---|---|
| ORCID iD | A researcher (a 16-character identifier) | Register once; attach to submissions, grants, datasets, and your CV; many venues and funders require it |
| DOI | A document or object | Assigned to articles by publishers; obtain your own for datasets, code, preprints, posters, and reports through a repository such as Zenodo or an institutional repository |
| ROR ID | A research organization | Choose affiliations from the submission system's list so that they resolve to the right organization |

Cite your own datasets and software by DOI in the paper, and archive the exact version used (a tagged release deposited in a repository), not a moving development branch.

## Open Access Routes

| Route | What it means | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Gold | The final article is open on the publisher's site under an open license | Often funded by an article processing charge; check for waivers and institutional agreements |
| Diamond (or platinum) | Open to read and free to publish | Funded by societies, universities, or consortia |
| Green | The author deposits the accepted manuscript in a repository | The publisher's policy sets which version, which license, and any embargo |
| Hybrid | A subscription journal makes individual articles open for a fee | Many funders do not pay for it or do not count it unless covered by an institutional agreement |
| Bronze | Free to read on the publisher's site without an open license | Can be withdrawn; usually does not satisfy a mandate |

- Know the three versions: submitted manuscript (preprint), accepted manuscript (after peer review, before typesetting), and version of record. Policies distinguish them.
- Keep the accepted manuscript file. Green deposit needs it, and it is often impossible to obtain later.
- Licenses: CC BY allows reuse with attribution and is what many funders require; more restrictive licenses limit reuse and may not comply.
- Before paying a charge, check whether the institution has an agreement with the publisher and whether the funder allows the cost.

## Funder Access and Data Mandates

Read the award terms. Typical requirements, which differ by funder and change over time:

- **Public access to publications.** Many funders require the accepted manuscript or the final article to be openly available on publication, without an embargo. In the United States, a 2022 White House Office of Science and Technology Policy memorandum (often called the Nelson Memo) asked federal agencies to do this; each agency wrote its own policy, the policies took effect in phases from 2024 to 2025, and they are still being revised, so read the funder's current text. In Europe and elsewhere, the funders in cOAlition S apply the Plan S principles (immediate open access under an open license, by an open-access venue or by repository deposit). Details such as eligible routes, licenses, and support for particular publishing agreements have changed over time: check the funder's current policy before choosing a venue.
- **Rights retention.** Some funders and institutions require authors to keep the right to deposit the accepted manuscript under an open license, and to say so at submission.
- **Data management and sharing.** A plan at application, and sharing of data underlying publications, with stated exceptions.
- **Acknowledgment** of the grant in a set form, with the grant number.
- **Identifiers,** such as an ORCID iD for the principal investigator.

A venue that cannot meet the funder's terms is the wrong venue, whatever its other merits.

## Cover Letters

A cover letter helps the editor decide whether to send the paper for review. Keep it under a page.

```text
Dear Dr. [Editor's name],

We submit "[Title]" for consideration as a [article type] in [Journal].

[Two or three sentences: the question, what was done, and the main finding,
with its key number.]

[Two or three sentences: why this matters to this journal's readers, and how
it relates to work the journal has published.]

[Required statements: the work is original and not under consideration
elsewhere; all authors approved the submission; preprint or prior presentation,
if any; competing interests; funding; ethics approval; data availability; use
of AI tools, if the journal asks for it here.]

[If requested: suggested reviewers with affiliation and expertise, and anyone
to exclude, with a brief professional reason.]

Sincerely,
[Corresponding author, affiliation, email, ORCID iD]
```

Do not paste the abstract, inflate novelty, or name reviewers with whom you have a conflict of interest.

## Submission and After

- Convert to the venue's template only after the content is verified, and recheck the result: conversion breaks citations, symbols, cross-references, and tables.
- Anonymize for double-anonymous review: names, affiliations, acknowledgments, self-citations phrased in the first person, file metadata, and repository links.
- Decisions: *minor revision* and *major revision* are invitations to continue; respond point by point (see `review-and-revision.md`). After a rejection, use the reviews to improve the paper before sending it to the next venue on the shortlist, and reformat for that venue.
- At proof stage, check author names and affiliations, numbers, equations, tables, figure quality, and references. Do not rewrite.
- After publication: deposit the permitted version, link the preprint, archive data and code with identifiers, and update your ORCID record.
- Keep an archive of the submitted and accepted files, the template version, the response letters, and the approval of all authors.
- If you find an error after publication, tell the journal and request a correction.

## Grant Proposals

A proposal argues that an important problem can be solved by this team, with this plan, for this money. Reviewers read many proposals quickly and score against published criteria.

### Principles

- **Write to the criteria.** Get the call text, the review criteria, and the scoring guide; use their terms as headings where the format allows. Obey every limit on pages, fonts, and sections; noncompliant proposals can be returned without review.
- **Put the point first** in every section, and make the first page carry the whole case.
- **Be concrete:** what will be done, by whom, when, and what will exist at the end.
- **Show feasibility:** preliminary results, access to data or sites, the team's record, and a realistic timeline.
- **Name the risks** and the alternative approach for each. Reviewers find risks anyway and reward applicants who planned for them.
- **Make aims independent enough** that failure of one does not sink the others.
- Write for an intelligent reviewer who is not a specialist in your subfield; panels are broad.
- Talk to the program officer where the funder invites it, and have someone outside the project read the draft against the criteria.

### The Aims or Summary Page

Reviewers often read this page first and most closely. A common one-page pattern:

1. The problem and why it matters, in two or three sentences.
2. What is known and the gap or barrier that blocks progress.
3. The long-term goal, the objective of this project, and the central hypothesis or question.
4. Why this team and approach: the rationale and preliminary evidence.
5. Two to four aims, each with a title that states an outcome, a sentence on the approach, and the expected result.
6. The payoff: what will be possible afterwards that is not possible now.

### Typical Structures

Check the funder's current guide; structures and review criteria are revised.

| Funder family | Core narrative |
|---|---|
| US National Institutes of Health research grants | A one-page Specific Aims, then a Research Strategy organized as Significance, Innovation, and Approach; reviewed, as of this writing (2025), under a simplified framework that scores Importance of the Research (significance and innovation), Rigor and Feasibility, and Expertise and Resources; check the current funding opportunity |
| US National Science Foundation | A one-page Project Summary with Overview, Intellectual Merit, and Broader Impacts, then a Project Description; proposals are judged on intellectual merit and broader impacts |
| European Union framework programs | Sections on excellence, impact, and quality and efficiency of implementation |
| Foundations and smaller funders | Often a letter of inquiry first, then need, objectives, methods, evaluation, budget, and sustainability |

### Common Sections

- **Significance or need:** the problem's importance, the specific gap, and what changes if the project succeeds.
- **Innovation:** what is new in concept, method, or application, stated against named current practice.
- **Approach,** for each aim: rationale, preliminary data, design and methods, analysis, expected outcomes, potential problems and alternatives, and milestones. State rigor explicitly: controls, sample-size reasoning, blinding, replication.
- **Timeline and milestones:** a simple chart with decision points.
- **Team and environment:** who does what, relevant record, facilities, collaborations with letters of support.
- **Broader impacts:** training, dissemination, benefits to society, and participation, with specific activities and how they will be assessed.
- **Data management and sharing plan,** and ethics or human-subjects sections, as required.

### Common Failings

The typical failures of proposals are listed in `publication-types.md`, Grant Proposal; review a draft against them before the reviewer simulation below.

### AI Tools and Confidentiality in Grants

Funders set their own rules on the use of generative AI in preparing applications, and several prohibit reviewers from using such tools on applications. Applicants remain responsible for originality and accuracy. Such rules can be strict and recent: NIH notice NOT-OD-25-132 (2025), for example, stated at the time of writing that applications substantially developed by AI will not be considered the applicants' original work, and it capped the number of applications per investigator per year. Check the funder's current policy, which may have changed, and do not upload other people's proposals or review materials to any external tool.

### Reviewer Simulation and Resubmission

- **Before submitting,** score the draft against the call's criteria as a panel member would, and list at least three concrete weaknesses, each with a fix. A reader outside the project does this best.
- **In a resubmission,** respond to every point of the summary statement or panel comments, in the funder's required format (often an introduction page), and say where the proposal changed; mark changes in the text where the funder allows. The response principles in `review-and-revision.md` apply: change the proposal, not only the reply, and disagree with evidence.

## Budget Justification

Justify each cost by the work it enables, in the funder's categories.

- **Personnel:** role, effort (percent of time or months), and what each person will do; salaries and benefits by institutional rates.
- **Equipment:** items above the sponsor's threshold, with why each is needed and why existing equipment will not do.
- **Supplies, travel, and other direct costs:** consumables, fieldwork, conference travel tied to dissemination, participant payments, publication and data-archiving costs, services.
- **Subawards and consultants:** scope of work and a budget for each.
- **Indirect costs** (facilities and administration): in US federal awards, the rate is negotiated between the institution and the government and applies to a base of modified total direct costs (MTDC) that excludes items such as equipment, tuition, participant support, and the part of each subaward above a threshold. The thresholds and definitions were revised in 2024 (as of this writing; check the current text), and sponsors can set their own limits. Get the current rate, base, and thresholds from the institution's sponsored-programs office; do not copy figures from an old proposal.

The budget, the timeline, and the aims must tell the same story: every aim is paid for, and every cost serves an aim.
