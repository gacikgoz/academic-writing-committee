# Integrity, Authorship, and AI Assistance

Authorship and contributor roles, the use and disclosure of AI tools, confidentiality, research integrity, open science, and reporting guidelines.

**Policies in this area change quickly.** This file states durable principles and names the bodies that set the rules. For anything a submission depends on (a journal's AI policy, a funder's mandate, a guideline's current version), read the current official text, and record the address and the date you read it. Where this file says "check the current policy," do that; do not rely on this file or on memory.

## What Must Never Be Invented

Never invent, guess, or auto-complete any of the following. Where the information is missing, write an explicit placeholder such as `[[TODO: ethics approval number]]`, or "not applicable," and resolve it with the person who knows.

- Citations, identifiers (DOI, PMID, ISBN, URL), page numbers, and quotations.
- Data, results, sample sizes, denominators, units, estimates, intervals, test statistics, and claims of significance.
- Methods, materials, protocol details, software versions, analysis choices, and deviations.
- Registrations, approvals, consent statements, participant details, and dates.
- Authors, author order, affiliations, contribution roles, acknowledgments, and permissions.
- Funding, grant numbers, sponsor roles, competing interests, data or code availability, and AI-use statements.

Keep a draft visibly marked as a draft until its placeholders are resolved. Polished formatting is not evidence that content has been checked.

## Authorship

- Use the target venue's authorship policy. Many journals, especially in biomedicine, follow the ICMJE recommendations, under which an author must meet all four criteria: (1) a substantial contribution to the conception or design of the work, or to the acquisition, analysis, or interpretation of data; (2) drafting the work or revising it critically; (3) approval of the final version; (4) agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work. Other fields use different norms (for example, alphabetical order in parts of mathematics and economics); follow the field and the venue.
- Everyone who qualifies is an author, and no one else is. Guest or gift authorship (listing someone who did not contribute) and ghost authorship (omitting someone who did) are both misconduct in most policies.
- Contributors who do not meet the criteria are acknowledged, with their consent.
- Agree on authorship, order, and the corresponding author early, in writing, and revisit when contributions change. Order conventions vary by field; do not infer contribution from position.
- Changes to the author list after submission need the agreement of all authors and usually the editor's approval.
- **An AI system cannot be an author.** It cannot approve a final version, be accountable, or declare conflicts, and in many jurisdictions it cannot hold copyright. ICMJE, COPE, and the major publishers take this position.

## Contributor Roles

The CRediT taxonomy (ANSI/NISO Z39.104-2022) describes who did what, in 14 roles: Conceptualization; Data curation; Formal analysis; Funding acquisition; Investigation; Methodology; Project administration; Resources; Software; Supervision; Validation; Visualization; Writing – original draft; Writing – review & editing.

- CRediT describes contributions. It does not decide who qualifies as an author.
- One person can hold several roles and one role can have several holders. Some venues ask for a degree of contribution (lead, equal, supporting).
- Many journals request or require a CRediT statement; adoption varies by publisher. Build the statement from records of what each person did, never from assumptions.
- An AI tool holds no role. If a tool helped with drafting (Writing – original draft), code (Software), or figures (Visualization), the person who used it and checked the output holds the role, and the use is disclosed separately.

## AI Assistance

### What Is Not Acceptable

- Listing an AI tool as an author or citing it as a source for a factual claim.
- Submitting AI-generated text, code, analysis, or images whose content no author has verified.
- Generated or altered data, results, or research images presented as obtained.
- Fabricated references (see `evidence-and-citations.md`).
- Substantial use that is not disclosed where the venue requires disclosure.
- Uploading confidential material (someone else's unpublished manuscript, a grant application under review, identifiable data) to a tool that is not authorized for it.

### What Is Commonly Accepted with Disclosure

Subject to the venue's and funder's current policy: language editing and translation of your own text; help with outlining and brainstorming; summarizing your own notes; drafting routine text that you then check and own; generating or reviewing analysis code that you test. Many venues do not require disclosure of basic spelling and grammar tools. Some restrict AI-generated images or particular article types. Check the current policy.

### Keeping a Record

For each tool, record as you go: name, provider, and version or date of use; what it was used for and at which stage; whether its output entered the text, code, analysis, tables, or figures; how a person verified that output; and what material was sent to it and under what authorization.

### Disclosure Statement

Use the venue's required wording and location. Policies commonly place writing assistance in the acknowledgments or a dedicated declaration, and use in data collection, analysis, or figure generation in the methods; some also ask for it in the cover letter or submission form. Where no wording is prescribed, adapt this:

> The authors used [tool name, with version or date of use] for [specific task, for example, improving the readability of Sections 2 and 3] while preparing this manuscript. They checked and edited all of its output and are responsible for the final content.

Name the tool and the specific task. Never write a disclosure, or a statement that no AI was used, without the authors' confirmation.

### Final Verification Checklist

- [ ] Read the venue's and the funder's current AI policies, and note the date.
- [ ] Every citation opened and checked: existence, authors, year, identifier, pages, and that it supports the claim.
- [ ] Every number, date, name, and quotation checked against its source or the analysis output.
- [ ] Code that an assistant wrote or changed has been read, tested, and rerun; reported numbers reproduce from the final code and data.
- [ ] The whole manuscript reread by the authors, so that the argument, emphasis, and voice are theirs.
- [ ] Disclosure written in the required form and place.

### AI-Text Detectors

Detectors of AI-generated text produce false positives, more often for nonnative writers of English and for formulaic technical prose, and false negatives for edited text. A detector score is never sufficient evidence of misconduct; at most it is a reason to talk with the author and look at drafts and version history.

## Confidentiality

- Unpublished manuscripts, review reports, editorial correspondence, grant applications under review, identifiable or sensitive data, and proprietary or contract-restricted material are confidential.
- Do not send such material to an external service, including an AI assistant, a translation or grammar tool, a plagiarism checker, or a search engine, unless the person or body entitled to authorize it has done so and the applicable policies (journal, funder, institution, consent, contract, law) allow it. A provider's promise not to train on the data is not authorization.
- When in doubt, work locally and use only the minimum necessary information. Removing names does not make data anonymous.
- The practical test: you can say exactly what was sent, to which service, under which terms, and who authorized it. If you cannot, do not send it.
- Reviewers and editors must not use what they learn from a submission before it is published, and must delete or return materials as the venue requires. Rules on AI use in peer review are in `review-and-revision.md`.

## Research Integrity

Codes such as the European Code of Conduct for Research Integrity (ALLEA) rest on reliability, honesty, respect, and accountability; national and institutional codes add their own procedures.

**Misconduct** in the strict sense is fabrication (making up data or results), falsification (manipulating materials, processes, data, or images, or omitting data, so that the record misrepresents the research), and plagiarism (using others' ideas, text, or results without credit). Honest error and differences of opinion are not misconduct.

**Questionable practices** that damage the record even when no rule names them:

- Running many analyses and reporting the one that reached significance (p-hacking); presenting a hypothesis formed after seeing the results as if it had been made beforehand (HARKing).
- Selective reporting of outcomes, conditions, or studies; dropping negative, null, or adverse findings.
- Splitting one study into minimal papers; duplicate publication; reusing your own published text or results without citation.
- Citation padding, coercive citation, and citing work you have not read.

**In writing, integrity means:**

- Report the whole record: primary and secondary outcomes; negative, null, adverse, and unexpected findings; missing data and attrition; deviations from the protocol or plan; failed or inconclusive experiments that bear on interpretation.
- Describe methods as performed and results as obtained. Keep an audit trail from raw data to each reported number.
- Label analyses by their true status: confirmatory, exploratory, or descriptive.
- Credit sources and collaborators fairly.
- Correct errors promptly and openly. After publication, do not edit the record silently: say what changed and why, link the correction to the original, and update data, code, and dependent claims.
- If you suspect misconduct or a serious error, preserve the records and follow the institution's, journal's, or funder's procedure. Do not investigate or accuse on your own.

## Ethics Approvals and Conflicts of Interest

- Before the research starts: ethics committee or institutional review board approval or exemption; informed consent procedures; protection of vulnerable groups; privacy and data security; animal welfare approvals; permissions for materials, sites, and community data.
- In the manuscript: the approving body and reference number, how consent was obtained or why it was waived, and trial or study registration, each stated only from the actual documents.
- Disclose financial and nonfinancial competing interests, funding sources, and the funder's role in design, analysis, writing, and the decision to publish. Disclosure is required even when you judge that the interest had no influence.

## Open Science and Reproducibility

- **Preregistration.** Register hypotheses, design, and analysis plan before data collection or before access to the data, on a registry suited to the field. It separates confirmatory from exploratory analysis; report deviations from it. Clinical trials generally must be registered before enrollment.
- **Registered reports.** Some journals review the introduction and methods before data collection and accept in principle regardless of the results. Check whether the target journal offers the format.
- **Data, code, and materials.** Share what the claims rest on, as far as consent, law, contracts, and community governance allow. Use a repository that assigns persistent identifiers and versions (institutional, general-purpose, or field-specific), choose a license, and document the files (data dictionary, units, software versions, how to run the code). The FAIR principles (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable) are the usual standard.
- **Availability statements** say what exists, where it is, under which license or access conditions, and how to request restricted material. "Available on request" is acceptable only if it describes a real, lasting process. Check that the deposit exists and matches the analysis before submitting.
- **A reproducibility package,** where permitted:
  - [ ] Registration or protocol, with deviations listed and explained.
  - [ ] Raw data, or pointers to it, and analysis-ready data with provenance.
  - [ ] Code with its environment (versions, dependencies) and instructions to run it.
  - [ ] Random seeds and any other settings needed to reproduce the numbers.
  - [ ] The tables behind each figure.
  - [ ] A map from each output to the claim in the paper that it supports.
- Never publish secrets, credentials, direct identifiers, or licensed material in a repository.
- Funders increasingly require data management and sharing plans and public access to outputs; see `publishing-and-grants.md` and check the current policy.

## Reporting Guidelines by Study Type

A reporting guideline lists what a report must contain for readers to appraise the study. It does not make a study rigorous, is not a quality score, and is not a risk-of-bias tool. Choose by the actual design and the kind of report, then get the current statement, checklist, and explanation document from the guideline's own site or the EQUATOR Network library (equator-network.org), and look for extensions that apply.

| Study or report type | Guideline to start from |
|---|---|
| Randomized trial, results | CONSORT (2025 update) |
| Trial protocol | SPIRIT (2025 update) |
| Systematic review and meta-analysis | PRISMA 2020; PRISMA-P for protocols; PRISMA-ScR for scoping reviews |
| Meta-analysis of observational studies | MOOSE |
| Cohort, case-control, cross-sectional study | STROBE, with extensions such as RECORD for routinely collected data |
| Diagnostic accuracy study | STARD; STARD-AI when an AI system is the index test |
| Prediction model development or validation | TRIPOD+AI; TRIPOD-LLM for studies of large language models |
| Trials and protocols of AI interventions | CONSORT-AI and SPIRIT-AI, with the current parent statements |
| Case report | CARE |
| Animal research | ARRIVE 2.0 |
| Qualitative research | SRQR; COREQ for interviews and focus groups |
| Quality improvement study | SQUIRE 2.0 |
| Economic evaluation | CHEERS 2022 |
| Omics, imaging, and other data-heavy fields | The field's minimum-information standard and the repository's metadata requirements |

Versions change; the table names the editions known when this file was written. If no guideline fits, say so instead of forcing the nearest one.

**Workflow.** Select the guideline while planning the study. Draft from the evidence. Complete the official checklist with the actual location of each item in the manuscript, mark missing items honestly, and submit the checklist and flow diagram if the venue asks. Recheck the version before submission.

**Common errors.** Claiming adherence after checking a few items; using a superseded version; omitting a relevant extension; ticking an item with no location; writing a missing method as if it had been done; treating a complete checklist as proof of quality.

## Declarations Checklist

Each item is either a statement verified against a record or an explicit "not applicable."

- [ ] Author list, order, affiliations, and corresponding author approved by all authors.
- [ ] Contribution statement (CRediT where used) built from records.
- [ ] Acknowledgments, with consent of those named.
- [ ] Funding, grant numbers, and the funder's role.
- [ ] Competing interests for every author.
- [ ] Ethics approval, consent, and registration, with identifiers.
- [ ] Data, code, materials, and protocol availability, with working identifiers.
- [ ] AI-use disclosure in the venue's form.
- [ ] Reporting-guideline checklist and flow diagram.
- [ ] Permissions for reused figures, tables, and long quotations.
- [ ] Statement that the work is original and not under review elsewhere, where requested; preprints and prior presentations declared.
